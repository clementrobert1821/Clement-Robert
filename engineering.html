Engineering Projects

Preparatory Classes Project – Analysis of Braking Systems in Roller Coasters

During my second year of preparatory classes, I worked on a two-person engineering project focused on braking mechanisms used in roller coasters. Our goal was to understand, model, and compare two major braking technologies: friction braking and eddy current (induction) braking.

My partner analysed induction braking, while I focused on friction braking. I studied how mechanical contact, materials, normal force, and heat generation influence braking efficiency. I developed simplified models to predict deceleration and compared them with experimental results.

To validate our work, we built a Lego inclined track on which a small cart could roll freely. At the end of the slope, we installed either friction pads or magnets to reproduce the two braking systems. This simple prototype allowed us to perform controlled measurements of braking distance, speed, and deceleration.

After analysing our datasets, we compared the performance, reliability, and limitations of both braking techniques.

This project strengthened my skills in experimental design, physics-based modelling, data analysis, and teamwork, and helped me develop a practical engineering mindset: designing a system, testing hypotheses, and confronting theory with real-world behaviour.

Electrodynamics Micro-project – Sizing and Study of an Autopilot Synchronous Machine

As part of my ENSEEIHT curriculum, I worked with a partner on the design and experimental study of a permanent magnet synchronous machine (PMSM).

Context & Objective: The goal was to define the geometric parameters of the machine to achieve a specific torque (0.075 N.m), and to analyse the switch control necessary for its autopilot operation (where the stator phase supply is synchronized with the actual rotor position using a sensor).

Method: We first performed theoretical sizing, applying Ampère's circuital law to calculate the magnetic field in the air gap (0.39 T) and derived the electromotive force (e.m.f.) and the required number of coil turns (30 turns). Following this, we conducted practical implementation on a 'Micromag' test bench, winding three phases shifted by 120°.

Results: We successfully demonstrated the autopilot principle. Experimental measurements using an oscilloscope confirmed a balanced three-phase e.m.f system. We also performed a power balance analysis, highlighting a very low efficiency (2.9%) mainly due to high Joule losses in the coils and mechanical friction in the test setup.

Acquired skills: Magnetic circuit modelling, electrical machine winding, oscilloscope measurements, power balance analysis, and understanding of inverter commutation sequences for synchronous motors.
